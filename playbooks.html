The one-page campaign brief

If you can't fill this in, the campaign isn't ready — and the missing box is usually the reason it will fail. Keep it to one page; a brief nobody reads is worse than no brief.

Campaign name:
Owner:
Dates: from — to

1. The one objective. One sentence, one number, one date. ("Book 40 qualified demos with mid-market finance teams by 30 June.")

2. Who exactly. The segment, the situation they're in, and roughly how many of them exist.

3. What they currently believe or do. The status quo you're trying to shift.

4. What we want them to believe or do instead. The single change.

5. The one thing to say. If they remember only one sentence, this is it.

6. Why they should believe it. Proof: data, customers, demo, guarantee.

7. Where. Channels, and why those and not others.

8. Budget and what it buys. Split by channel with the expected outcome per channel.

9. How we'll know it worked. Primary metric, the counter-metric that stops us gaming it, and the measurement method.

10. What would make us stop. The kill criteria, agreed now, while nobody is emotionally invested.

11. Mandatories and constraints. Legal, brand, regulatory, technical.

Box 10 is the one everyone skips

Agreeing kill criteria before launch is the single cheapest discipline in marketing. Afterwards, sunk cost and ego make the conversation almost impossible, and campaigns limp on for months because nobody wants to be the one who calls it.

Product launch checklist

Ordered by when you should do it, not by importance.

4–6 weeks out

  • Positioning statement agreed and written down by one person
  • Target segment named, with a rough count of how many exist
  • Pricing and packaging locked
  • Success metric and kill criteria agreed
  • Interview 5 target customers on the message before building assets
  • Competitive teardown refreshed

2–3 weeks out

  • Landing page live and tested on a real phone
  • Analytics, events and conversion goals firing correctly
  • Sales enablement: deck, one-pager, objection handling, pricing rules
  • Support briefed with expected questions and answers
  • Email sequence written and scheduled
  • Press or creator outreach started (they need lead time)

Launch week

  • Announcement email to existing customers before anyone else
  • Social and community posts staggered, not all at once
  • Paid campaigns live with conversion tracking verified
  • Someone on standby to answer replies the same day
  • A rollback plan if something breaks

2–4 weeks after

  • Measure against the metric agreed in advance — not a new one that looks better
  • Collect objections sales actually heard and update the messaging
  • Interview 3 people who bought, and 3 who looked and didn't
  • Write down what you'd do differently, while it's still fresh
  • Decide: double down, adjust, or stop
Launches are not campaigns

A launch is a day; demand generation is a year. The most common post-launch mistake is treating the spike as the result, then moving on. The spike mostly measures how good your existing audience is — the real question is what happens in month three, when nobody is announcing anything.

Landing page audit

Work down the list. Most pages fail in the first three items, and no amount of button-colour testing will rescue them.

Clarity

  • A stranger can say what this is and who it's for within 5 seconds
  • The headline names an outcome, not a feature or a category
  • The page matches the promise of the ad or link that brought them
  • One primary action; everything else is visibly secondary
  • No unexplained jargon or invented product names above the fold

Persuasion

  • Specific proof: numbers, named customers, screenshots, a real demo
  • The top 3–5 objections are answered on the page
  • Pricing is visible, or the reason it isn't is credible
  • Risk reducers present: trial, guarantee, easy cancellation, security
  • Social proof is near the action, not stranded in a carousel

Friction

  • Form asks only for what you genuinely need now
  • Every extra field is justified by someone out loud
  • No forced account creation before value
  • Errors are inline, specific and human
  • Works with autofill and password managers

Technical

  • Tested on a real mid-range phone, on mobile data, not just a desktop resize
  • Largest Contentful Paint under ~2.5s; layout doesn't jump as it loads
  • Tap targets comfortable; nothing requires a pinch-zoom
  • Keyboard navigable; images have alt text; contrast passes
  • Conversion tracking verified by completing a real test conversion
The five-second test

Show the page to someone unfamiliar for five seconds, then hide it and ask: what does this company do, who is it for, and what did they want you to do? It costs nothing, takes two minutes, and finds bigger problems than a month of A/B tests. Do it before you touch anything else.

Email deliverability checklist

Deliverability is invisible until it collapses, and by then it takes months to repair. Check these quarterly.

Authentication

  • SPF record published and lists your sending service
  • DKIM signing enabled and verified
  • DMARC policy published — required by major providers for bulk senders
  • Sending from a subdomain (mail.yourdomain.com), keeping your root domain's reputation separate
  • Reverse DNS and a consistent "from" address

List practice

  • Every address gave permission — no purchased or scraped lists, ever
  • New domains warmed gradually over 2–4 weeks
  • Hard bounces removed immediately
  • Non-openers suppressed after 3–6 months
  • Re-engagement campaign before sunsetting a segment

Content & compliance

  • One-click unsubscribe, honoured immediately
  • Physical postal address in the footer where required
  • Sensible text-to-image ratio; a plain-text alternative included
  • Link shorteners avoided; links point at your own domain
  • Consent basis documented — GDPR, CAN-SPAM, CASL and local rules differ, so check yours

Monitoring

  • Spam complaint rate tracked, kept well under 0.3%
  • Postmaster or sender-reputation tools connected
  • Inbox placement spot-checked across the major providers
  • Blocklist monitoring in place
  • Bounce and complaint rates reviewed per send, not per quarter

Creative testing framework

Most "creative testing" is twenty variations of the same idea. Test concepts first, then refine within the winner.

Concept angles worth having in rotation

Problem agitation · Before and after · Direct demonstration · Customer testimonial · Founder to camera · Comparison against the status quo · Objection handled head-on · Unexpected use case · Data or original research · Anti-pitch ("don't buy this if…"). Keep several live at once — the winner is rarely the one you expected, and that is the entire point of testing.

Running a customer interview

Five conversations will teach you more than a thousand-response survey, provided you resist the urge to pitch.

Rules

  • Ask about the past, not the future. "Would you use this?" produces polite lies. "What did you do last time?" produces facts.
  • Don't describe your solution until the end, if at all.
  • Chase the story, not the opinion. Specific events beat general preferences every time.
  • Get comfortable with silence. The useful answer usually comes after the first pause.
  • Record their words verbatim. Customer phrasing is the best ad copy you will ever write, and paraphrasing destroys it.

A question sequence

  1. "Walk me through the last time you dealt with [problem]."
  2. "What did you try? How did that go?"
  3. "What made you start looking for something new?"
  4. "What did you search for, and who did you ask?"
  5. "Who else had to be convinced?"
  6. "What almost stopped you from going ahead?"
  7. "If this disappeared tomorrow, what would you do?"
  8. "Who else should I talk to about this?"

Operating cadence

Most marketing teams either review nothing or review everything weekly. Match the review rhythm to how fast the metric can actually move.

RhythmLook atDecide
Daily 5 min Spend pacing, anything broken, tracking still firing Nothing strategic. Only: is something on fire?
Weekly 45 min Leads or orders vs target, CPA by channel, creative performance, test results that have reached their sample size Shift budget between channels, kill fatigued creative, launch the next test
Monthly 90 min Blended CAC and MER, cohort retention, funnel step conversion, pipeline, content and SEO trend Channel mix, hiring and agency needs, what to stop doing
Quarterly Half day LTV:CAC and payback, brand and share-of-search trend, competitive shifts, what we learned Strategy, positioning, next quarter's bets, budget reallocation
Annually Positioning, segments, pricing, brand/performance split, channel portfolio The things that are too disruptive to change more often

Don't judge slow metrics on a fast clock

Reviewing SEO weekly or brand monthly guarantees you'll kill something that was working. The noise at those intervals is larger than the signal, so you'll be reacting to randomness — and you'll feel decisive while doing it.

First 90 days in a new marketing role

Days 1–30

Learn, don't launch

  • Talk to 10 customers and 5 people who chose a competitor
  • Sit in on sales calls and read support tickets
  • Map the existing funnel with real numbers, however ugly
  • Audit what's running now and what it costs, fully loaded
  • Find out how the company actually makes money, from finance
  • Ship one small visible fix so you build credit early
Days 31–60

Focus and fix

  • Write the positioning down; get explicit agreement on it
  • Fix the biggest measurement gap — you can't manage what you can't see
  • Stop two things that aren't working; say so publicly
  • Pick one or two channels to genuinely commit to
  • Set the metric, the counter-metric and the reporting cadence
Days 61–90

Build the engine

  • Run the first proper tests with pre-agreed sample sizes
  • Build one repeatable process (content, lifecycle, or creative)
  • Present a 2–3 quarter plan with explicit assumptions and kill criteria
  • Name what you're deliberately not doing, and why
The temptation to resist

New marketers are expected to produce visible activity fast, so they launch campaigns in week two against a funnel they don't understand. The activity buys you thirty days of goodwill and costs you the quarter. Learning first looks slower and is almost always faster.
