What marketing actually is

Marketing is the work of making a product easy to find, easy to choose, and easy to keep buying. Advertising is one tactic inside that. Pricing, packaging, distribution, positioning and retention are all marketing decisions too, and they usually move the number more than the ads do.

Two definitions worth holding in tension:

  • Kotler's classical view — marketing identifies unmet needs, quantifies them, and directs the organisation to serve them profitably. Marketing is upstream of the product.
  • Ehrenberg-Bass empirical view — brands grow mainly by increasing penetration (more buyers), and buying decisions are driven by mental availability (you come to mind) and physical availability (you're easy to buy). Marketing is largely a memory and distribution problem.
A question to keep asking

When growth stalls, is the problem that people don't know you (awareness), don't get you (positioning), don't believe you (proof), can't easily buy you (friction), or don't stay (product)? These five have completely different fixes, and only one of them is solved by more ads.

The 4 Ps — and the 7 Ps for services

McCarthy's marketing mix. Old, sometimes sneered at, still the fastest checklist for "what could we actually change?"

PThe decisionQuestions that expose gaps
ProductWhat you sell, and the shape it takesWhat job does it do better than the alternative? What could we remove without anyone noticing?
PriceWhat you charge and how you charge itAre we priced against a competitor or against the value of the outcome? What is our discount discipline?
PlaceWhere it can be boughtHow many steps from "interested" to "paid"? Which channel do our buyers already trust?
PromotionHow you communicate itWould a stranger understand what we sell in five seconds? Do we look like us, or like the category?

Marketing textbooks also teach a customer-side mirror — the 4 Cs: Customer value, Cost to the customer, Convenience, Communication. Same decisions, phrased from outside the company. If your 4 Ps answers sound self-serving, re-answer them as 4 Cs.

STP: segmentation, targeting, positioning

The standard sequence for deciding who you are for. Most "our marketing isn't working" problems are actually a skipped step here.

Step 1

Segmentation

Split the market into groups that behave differently. Common bases:

  • Demographic — age, income, job title, company size
  • Geographic — country, city, climate, density
  • Behavioural — usage rate, purchase trigger, loyalty
  • Psychographic — values, attitudes, identity
  • Needs-based — the job they are hiring you for

Behavioural and needs-based segments predict buying far better than demographic ones. "Marketing managers aged 30–45" is a description; "teams who just lost their analyst and inherited a reporting mess" is a segment.

Step 2

Targeting

Choose which segments to serve. Score each on:

  • Size and growth
  • Reachability — can you actually get in front of them affordably?
  • Willingness and ability to pay
  • Competitive intensity
  • Fit with what you're genuinely good at

Reachability is the one people forget. A perfect segment you cannot cheaply reach is worse than a decent one you can.

Step 3

Positioning

Decide the place you want to occupy in that segment's mind, relative to their alternatives — including the alternative of doing nothing.

Positioning is a choice about what you give up. If your positioning statement would also be true of three competitors, you haven't positioned; you've described the category.

The mass-market caveat

Tight targeting is right when your budget is small or your product is genuinely specialised. But the Ehrenberg-Bass evidence is that large brands grow by reaching all category buyers, not by narrowing. Tight targeting is often a budget constraint dressed up as a strategy — useful, but know which one you're doing.

Writing a positioning statement

One sentence, decided once, then used everywhere: the website headline, the sales deck, the ad copy, the onboarding email. If four people in your company write it differently, that is your real marketing problem.

Template

For [target segment] who [situation or unmet need], [product] is the [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [main alternative], we [the differentiator you can prove].

Worked example

Illustrative only — a fictional product, written to show the shape of a good answer.

For small accounting firms who lose billable hours chasing clients for documents, Ledgerly is the client-request tool that collects every document with automatic reminders. Unlike email and shared drives, we show partners exactly which client is holding up which return.

How to pressure-test it

  • The competitor test. Swap in a competitor's name. Still true? Then it isn't positioning.
  • The opposite test. Would any sane company claim the opposite? "We have great customer service" fails; nobody claims bad service. "We only support firms under 20 people" passes.
  • The sales-call test. Say it out loud to a prospect. If you find yourself adding "what I mean is…", rewrite it.
  • The proof test. Can you evidence the differentiator today, without a roadmap promise?

Jobs to be done

People don't buy products; they hire them to make progress in a situation. The unit of analysis is the circumstance, not the customer.

The framing that makes it usable: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]."

Three forces to map

Push
What's frustrating about the current situation
Pull
What's attractive about the new solution
Anxiety & habit
Fear of the new thing, plus inertia of the old one

Most marketing over-invests in pull. Switching usually stalls on anxiety and habit — so case studies, migration help, free trials and money-back guarantees often move more revenue than another feature benefit.

Interview questions that work

  • "Take me back to the day you decided to look for something. What happened?"
  • "What did you type into the search bar?"
  • "Who else had to agree?"
  • "What were you using before, and what did you do with it after?"
  • "What almost stopped you from buying?"
Why this beats personas

A persona tells you the buyer drinks oat lattes. A job tells you they searched at 11pm the night their spreadsheet broke. Only one of those tells you what to write, where to put it, and when.

Funnel and journey models

Four models for the same underlying thing. Pick by what you need to decide, not by fashion.

ModelStagesBest used forWeakness
AIDA Attention → Interest → Desire → Action Writing a single piece of copy or an ad Ends at purchase; ignores retention entirely
Classic funnel Awareness → Consideration → Conversion → Retention → Advocacy Budget allocation and org-wide reporting Implies a tidy one-way journey that almost nobody takes
AARRR ("pirate metrics") Acquisition → Activation → Retention → Referral → Revenue Product-led and subscription businesses Very product-centric; weak on brand and demand creation
See–Think–Do–Care Largest audience → researching → ready to buy → existing customers Matching content and bids to intent Intent is harder to observe than the model assumes

What every funnel model gets wrong

Real buying is loopy and mostly invisible. People leave, return months later, ask a colleague, read a review you'll never see, and arrive via a branded search that gets all the credit. Use funnels to find leaks and allocate budget. Don't use them to claim you know the path any individual took.

Brand vs performance marketing

Not opposites — different time horizons. Performance harvests demand that already exists. Brand creates the demand that performance will harvest in six months.

Brand buildingPerformance / activation
TargetsEveryone who might ever buy the categoryPeople showing buying signals now
Works overMonths to yearsDays to weeks
EffectRaises the baseline; reduces price sensitivitySharp spike, then decays quickly
Typical measuresShare of search, brand recall, direct traffic, pricing powerROAS, CPA, conversion rate, MER
MeasurementHard, slow, needs holdouts or geo testsEasy, fast, and prone to over-crediting itself

The trap

Performance is measurable, so it wins every budget argument — and it will keep winning right up until the demand it harvests runs out. The warning sign is rising CPA across every channel at once, with no obvious auction change. That usually means the top of the funnel was starved 6–12 months ago.

Market analysis frameworks

SWOT

Strengths and Weaknesses are internal and present-tense. Opportunities and Threats are external and future-tense. Mixing those up is why most SWOTs are useless.

Make it work: allow a maximum of three items per box, require evidence for each, and finish by writing one action per quadrant. A SWOT with no actions is a list, not an analysis.

Porter's five forces

Explains why a market is or isn't profitable:

  1. Threat of new entrants
  2. Bargaining power of suppliers
  3. Bargaining power of buyers
  4. Threat of substitutes
  5. Rivalry among existing competitors

PESTEL

Scans the macro environment: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal. Most useful for regulated categories and for annual planning — it catches things like a privacy law change that quietly deletes a third of your targeting.

Competitive teardown

More useful than any acronym, and almost nobody does it properly. For each of your top three competitors, record: their positioning line, their pricing and packaging, their top landing page, their main paid keywords, their review complaints, their hiring pattern.

Review complaints are the richest source. A competitor's one-star reviews are a list of promises you could credibly make.

Value proposition & messaging hierarchy

Positioning is internal strategy. The value proposition is the customer-facing expression of it. Build it in layers so every asset pulls from the same source.

LayerWhat it isWhere it appears
PromiseThe single outcome you're accountable forHomepage headline, ad hook
PillarsThree reasons the promise is credibleSection headers, deck slides
ProofEvidence per pillar: numbers, customers, demos, guaranteesCase studies, logos, screenshots
Objection handlingThe five reasons people don't buy, answeredFAQ, pricing page, sales follow-up

Benefit > feature, but specificity > both

"Save time" is a benefit and it is worthless — everyone says it. "Close the month in two days instead of nine" is a benefit with a number attached, and it is the whole ad. If you can't put a number on it, ask a customer; they usually can.

Pricing

The fastest lever on profit and the least-tested part of most marketing mixes. A 1% price increase drops almost entirely to the bottom line; a 1% volume increase does not.

ApproachHow the price is setRisk
Cost-plusUnit cost plus a target marginIgnores what the customer would have paid; leaves money on the table for high-value products
CompetitiveAnchored to rivalsOutsources your strategy to whoever is most desperate
Value-basedA share of the economic value the buyer gainsRequires real research; hard for undifferentiated products
PenetrationDeliberately low to buy share fastAttracts price-sensitive churners; raising prices later is painful
SkimmingHigh at launch, lowered over timeInvites competitors in; annoys early adopters
FreemiumFree tier, paid upgradeOnly works if free users convert or create distribution; otherwise it's a support cost

Psychological effects worth knowing

  • Anchoring — the first number shown reframes every number after it. This is why an expensive tier exists.
  • Decoy effect — a deliberately poor-value middle option makes the target option look rational.
  • Charm pricing — prices ending in 9 lift volume in consumer categories and can cheapen perception in premium ones.
  • Bundling — hides individual price comparison and raises average order value; also hides which part customers actually value.
  • Good–better–best — most buyers avoid extremes, so the middle tier is where you should put your intended price.

Test pricing carefully. Live price tests can be unfair to customers and are legally sensitive in some markets. Safer routes: test on new customers only, use geographic splits, grandfather existing customers, or run Van Westendorp / conjoint surveys before touching the live page.

Product–market fit signals

No marketing budget survives a product people don't want. Before scaling spend, look for evidence — not vibes.

Signals it exists

  • Retention curves flatten rather than decaying to zero
  • Organic and word-of-mouth signups grow without spend
  • Over ~40% of users say they'd be "very disappointed" if the product vanished (Sean Ellis survey)
  • Sales cycles shorten; people ask "how do I buy" before you pitch
  • Customers use it in ways you didn't plan for

Signals it doesn't

  • Growth stops the week you stop spending
  • Every deal needs a discount or a custom feature
  • Churn is concentrated in months 1–3 — people never got value
  • Usage is broad but shallow; nobody has a weekly habit
  • Your case studies are all from friends of the founders
Before you scale spend

Scaling acquisition on a leaky product converts budget into churned customers and a damaged brand at the same time. If month-3 retention is bad, fixing it usually returns more than any campaign you could run with the same money — and it makes every future campaign work better.
