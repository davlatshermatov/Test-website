How to choose channels

The most reliable predictor of which channels work is how much a customer is worth and how they decide. Match the cost of the channel to the value of the customer.

Average customer valueChannels that usually pay backChannels that usually don't
Under ~$100 consumer, one-off Paid social, SEO, email, referral, marketplaces, creator content Events, field sales, direct mail, high-touch anything
~$100–$1,000 subscription, SMB Paid search, paid social, SEO, content, lifecycle email, affiliate Conferences, enterprise ABM, outbound SDR teams
~$1,000–$25,000 B2B mid-market SEO, content, paid search, webinars, review sites, partnerships, targeted paid social Mass-reach TV, broad OOH, untargeted display
Over ~$25,000 enterprise Events, ABM, direct mail, analyst relations, executive content, outbound Anything optimised for cheap clicks; CPA bidding on a 9-month sales cycle

Four questions before adding a channel

  1. Is the demand already there, or do I have to create it? Search captures. Social and video create. Confusing the two is why "we tried Google Ads and nobody searched for us" happens.
  2. Can I afford to wait? SEO and community compound but pay nothing for months. If you need revenue this quarter, they're the wrong first move regardless of their long-term ROI.
  3. Will I have enough volume to learn? Below roughly 30–50 conversions per month, a channel can't be optimised — you'll be reading noise. Consolidate budget rather than spreading it.
  4. Who owns it? A channel without a named owner and weekly time allocated to it will produce nothing. Two well-run channels beat six neglected ones, every time.
A sequencing pattern that usually works

Start with one capture channel that proves people want this (usually paid search or a marketplace). Add one owned channel to keep the people you've already paid for (email). Only then add a creation channel to grow the pool. Most teams do this in exactly the wrong order.

Search & content

SEO

Three parts, and almost everyone over-invests in the first:

Technical
Crawlable, fast, indexable, sensible site structure
On-page
Content that matches what the searcher actually wants
Off-page
Links and mentions that signal you're worth ranking

Search intent is the whole game. Ranking for "best CRM" needs a comparison page, not a product page. Look at what already ranks — the results are the answer key for what the engine thinks the intent is.

Content marketing

Content fails for one of three reasons, in this order of frequency:

  1. No distribution. Publishing is not distributing. Budget as much effort for promotion as for creation.
  2. No point of view. Summarising what everyone else already said ranks nowhere and convinces no one.
  3. No commercial connection. Traffic on topics your product can't help with is a cost centre.

Rank content by business potential, not search volume: can you naturally mention your product in this article? If no, it's brand work — fund it as brand work, and measure it as brand work.

The keyword tiering that keeps SEO honest

Tier 1 — buying intent ("pricing", "alternatives", "vs", "best X for Y"): low volume, high value, write these first. Tier 2 — problem-aware ("how to fix X"): medium volume, your product is a natural answer. Tier 3 — top of funnel ("what is X"): huge volume, rarely converts, cheap to produce, easy to over-invest in. Most blogs are 90% Tier 3 and then wonder why SEO "doesn't drive revenue".

Paid media

Paid search

Structure: separate brand and non-brand campaigns always. Blending them produces a flattering, useless average — brand terms convert at multiples of non-brand and will hide a failing prospecting campaign indefinitely.

Match types: broad match plus smart bidding works when you have conversion volume and solid negative keyword hygiene. With low volume, tighter match types and manual control usually waste less.

Paid social

Creative is the targeting. Algorithms now find the audience; your creative decides who self-selects. Testing five audiences with one ad is usually the wrong experiment — test five ads on one broad audience.

Plan for fatigue. Performance decays as frequency rises against the same pool. Keep a creative pipeline; "the ad stopped working" usually means "the audience has seen it eleven times".

Retargeting

The most over-credited spend in marketing. You are advertising to people already familiar with you — a large share would have returned anyway, and the platform reports every one of them as a win. Cap frequency, exclude recent purchasers, and periodically run a holdout to see what the true incremental effect is. It is often a fraction of what the dashboard claims.

Budget discipline

  • Change one major variable at a time, or you'll learn nothing
  • Give an algorithm its learning period before judging it — usually 1–2 weeks or ~50 conversions
  • Scale by 20–30% increments, not doubles, unless you want to reset learning
  • Watch blended CAC alongside platform CPA; when they diverge, the platform is taking credit for organic demand

Owned & lifecycle

Email: the automations worth building first

  1. Welcome series — typically the highest-performing emails you'll ever send; the moment of maximum interest
  2. Abandoned cart / abandoned checkout — the single biggest ecommerce revenue automation
  3. Browse abandonment — softer intent, still profitable
  4. Post-purchase — sets expectations, reduces refunds, earns reviews
  5. Winback — cheaper than acquiring someone new
  6. Replenishment or renewal reminders — pure retained revenue where the product suits it

Deliverability, in short

  • Authenticate properly: SPF, DKIM and DMARC — now effectively mandatory for bulk senders at the major inbox providers
  • Warm new sending domains gradually; don't blast a cold list on day one
  • Never buy a list. It will damage the sending reputation of every campaign that follows
  • Actively suppress people who haven't opened or clicked in 3–6 months — engagement is a ranking signal
  • Make unsubscribing one click; hiding it drives spam complaints, which cost far more
Open rates are not what they were

Since Apple's Mail Privacy Protection (2021), a large share of "opens" are pre-fetched by the provider, not by a human. Open rate is now only useful as a relative signal between similar sends. Optimise for clicks and revenue per recipient, and treat any year-on-year open-rate comparison that crosses 2021 with suspicion.

Earned & community

Influencer & creator

Vet on engagement quality and audience overlap, not follower count. Smaller creators often deliver better cost per acquisition and more genuine trust.

  • Ask for audience demographics and recent post screenshots before paying
  • Sudden follower spikes or generic comments are warning signs
  • Give creative freedom — scripted brand copy reads as an ad and performs like one
  • Use unique codes or links so you can measure something
  • Disclosure is a legal requirement in most markets, not a nice-to-have. Put it in the contract

PR & earned media

A journalist needs a story, not an announcement. Things that are actually stories: original data you collected, a genuine conflict or trend, a surprising outcome, a named person doing something.

Measure PR as branded search volume and direct traffic in the days after a hit. Clip counts and "advertising value equivalent" are theatre.

Community

The slowest channel and the stickiest. It pays back in retention and support deflection long before it pays back in acquisition. It needs one accountable owner, a reason for members to talk to each other rather than to you, and about a year of patience. Don't start one to hit a quarterly number.

Referral programmes

Two-sided rewards (both referrer and friend get something) generally outperform one-sided. The real constraints are:

  • Ask at a moment of proven satisfaction, not at signup
  • Make the reward relevant to the product, not a generic gift card, if you want quality referrals
  • Reduce sharing to one tap
  • If people won't refer you for free, a reward mostly buys fraud

Offline

Events & field marketing

The booth is rarely the expensive part once you count flights, hotels, staff days and swag. Before committing:

  • Get the attendee profile, not just the attendee count
  • Decide the follow-up sequence before the event — most event ROI is lost in the week after
  • Book meetings in advance; walk-up traffic is a bonus, not a plan
  • Track pipeline sourced and influenced, over the full sales cycle

Direct mail & out-of-home

Both trade measurability for cut-through. Both are testable if you're disciplined:

  • Geo holdouts — run in some regions, hold others back, compare total sales. This is the only trustworthy read on OOH
  • Unique URLs, QR codes and phone numbers — undercounts real response, but gives a floor
  • Matched-market tests — pair similar cities, vary one, measure the difference
  • For direct mail, list quality outweighs creative. A great piece to a bad list fails every time

Attribution: what you can and can't know

Every channel section above assumes you can tell what worked. Mostly, you can't — not precisely. Here is the honest hierarchy of methods.

MethodWhat it tells youTrust level
Platform-reported conversionsWhat the platform wants credit forLow — self-reported, double-counts across platforms
Last-click analyticsThe final touch before purchaseLow — systematically over-credits branded search and retargeting
Multi-touch modelsA weighted story across touchpointsMedium — blind to everything untracked: word of mouth, offline, dark social

The sum-greater-than-the-whole test

Add up the conversions each platform claims. If the total exceeds the orders in your actual accounting system — and it usually will, often by 30–60% — every platform number you're optimising against is inflated. The fix isn't a better model; it's to anchor on blended CAC (total marketing spend ÷ total new customers) and use platform data only for relative decisions within that channel.
