Section 03

Metrics & formulas

Every metric with its formula, a realistic range, and — more usefully — the specific way it lies to you.

Before you optimise anything

A metric is only useful if you'd act differently at different values of it. Write down, in advance, what you'd do if the number were 20% better and 20% worse. If the answer is "nothing" in both directions, stop tracking it on your dashboard — you have a report, not a metric.

Acquisition & media efficiency

CPM — cost per mille

What it costs to show your ad 1,000 times

CPM = (Total spend ÷ Impressions) × 1,000

Watch out: a low CPM usually means a low-quality placement. Compare CPM only within the same placement type, and prefer viewable CPM (vCPM) where you can get it — an impression nobody could see costs the same as one they could.

CPC — cost per click

CPC = Total spend ÷ Clicks

Watch out: CPC is an input, not an outcome. Cutting CPC by chasing cheap traffic almost always raises CPA. The only CPC that matters is the one attached to traffic that converts.

CTR — click-through rate

CTR = (Clicks ÷ Impressions) × 100

Watch out: the fastest way to raise CTR is to write a misleading hook, which raises bounce and lowers conversion. Judge CTR and conversion rate together, never CTR alone.

CPA / CPL — cost per acquisition or lead

CPA = Campaign spend ÷ Conversions

Watch out: "conversion" must be defined identically across channels or the comparison is meaningless. A newsletter signup and a booked demo are not the same conversion, yet they are routinely averaged into one CPA.

CAC — customer acquisition cost

The most argued-about number in marketing

CAC = Total sales & marketing cost ÷ New customers acquired

Three versions, and you should know which one is being quoted:

Paid CAC
Ad spend ÷ customers attributed to ads. Flattering; ignores salaries and organic.
Blended CAC
All marketing spend ÷ all new customers. Honest, hard to game, the one to report to a board.
Fully-loaded CAC
Adds salaries, tools, agency fees and commissions. The true cost of growth.

Watch out: blended CAC looks great when organic is carrying you, which hides a paid channel that has stopped working. Track both, and watch the gap between them over time.

ROAS — return on ad spend

ROAS = Revenue attributable to ads ÷ Ad spend

Watch out: ROAS uses revenue, not profit. A 3× ROAS on a product with a 25% gross margin loses money on every order. Use break-even ROAS as the bar:

Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ Gross margin %

At a 40% margin, break-even ROAS is 2.5×. Anything under that is buying revenue at a loss — which is occasionally a deliberate strategy, and more often an accident.

MER — marketing efficiency ratio

Also called blended ROAS

MER = Total revenue ÷ Total marketing spend

Why it matters: MER is attribution-proof. It doesn't care which platform claims what — it compares everything you spent to everything you sold. It's blunt, and increasingly it's the number growth teams steer by.

POAS — profit on ad spend

POAS = Gross profit from ads ÷ Ad spend

The version of ROAS that survives contact with a finance team. Essential where margins differ a lot by product — otherwise your ad algorithm happily optimises toward your worst-margin SKU.

Reach & frequency

Frequency = Impressions ÷ Reach

Rule of thumb: effective frequency for a new message is usually a few exposures; beyond roughly 8–10 in a short window you're mostly buying irritation. If reach is flat and frequency is climbing, you've saturated the audience — widen it or refresh the creative.

Conversion & revenue

CVR — conversion rate

CVR = (Conversions ÷ Sessions or visitors) × 100

Watch out: sessions and users give different denominators and therefore different rates. Pick one, document it, and never change it mid-comparison. Also segment by device — a blended rate hides a broken mobile checkout for months.

AOV — average order value

AOV = Total revenue ÷ Number of orders

Watch out: the mean is dragged around by outliers. Check the median too. Raising AOV through bundles and thresholds ("free shipping over $50") is usually cheaper than raising traffic.

RPV — revenue per visitor

RPV = Total revenue ÷ Total visitors  (= CVR × AOV)

The best single ecommerce headline metric, because it can't be gamed by improving one half at the expense of the other. A "winning" test that lifts conversion but sinks AOV shows up here immediately.

Cart & checkout abandonment

Cart abandonment % = (1 − Completed purchases ÷ Carts created) × 100

Consistently high across the industry — roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of carts. The recoverable portion is usually lost to unexpected shipping costs, forced account creation, a clunky payment step, or missing payment options. Fix those before writing another abandonment email.

Lead-to-customer rate & win rate

Lead-to-customer % = (Customers ÷ Leads) × 100
Win rate % = (Deals won ÷ Deals closed) × 100

Watch out: win rate is measured on closed deals, not on all opportunities — otherwise open deals suppress it artificially. A rising lead count with a falling lead-to-customer rate means marketing is buying volume at the expense of quality.

Pipeline coverage

Coverage = Open pipeline value ÷ Revenue target for the period

B2B teams commonly aim for 3–4× coverage. It's a planning tool, not a truth: coverage built from unqualified opportunities is a story told with big numbers.

Sales cycle length

Cycle length = Total days from first touch to close ÷ Deals closed

Use the median, not the mean — one nine-month enterprise deal distorts an average built from three-week deals. Cycle length is also the reason short-window ad attribution fails in B2B: the conversion arrives long after the tracking window closed.

Retention & customer value

Churn rate

Customer churn % = (Customers lost in period ÷ Customers at start) × 100
Revenue churn % = (Revenue lost in period ÷ Revenue at start) × 100

Watch out: customer churn and revenue churn diverge sharply when your small accounts leave and your large ones stay — or vice versa. Report both. Annual churn is not monthly churn × 12; compounding makes the real figure lower.

NRR & GRR — net and gross revenue retention

NRR = (Starting revenue + expansion − contraction − churn) ÷ Starting revenue × 100
GRR = (Starting revenue − contraction − churn) ÷ Starting revenue × 100

NRR above 100% means you'd grow even if you never acquired another customer. GRR strips out expansion and shows whether you're actually keeping people — a healthy NRR sitting on top of a poor GRR means a few big upgrades are masking a leaky base.

Repeat purchase rate & purchase frequency

Repeat rate % = (Customers with 2+ orders ÷ Total customers) × 100
Purchase frequency = Total orders ÷ Unique customers (in a period)

For ecommerce, the second order is the hinge. The probability of a third order is far higher than the probability of a second — so concentrate lifecycle effort on getting people from one to two.

LTV / CLV — customer lifetime value

Two formulas; use the second one

Simple: LTV = AOV × Purchase frequency × Customer lifespan
Better: LTV = (AOV × Frequency × Lifespan) × Gross margin %
Subscription: LTV = (ARPA × Gross margin %) ÷ Churn rate

Watch out: revenue-based LTV overstates value by exactly your cost of goods, and LTV is a forecast dressed as a fact. A young company projecting a five-year lifespan from four months of data is guessing. Where you can, use a 12-month realised value instead — it's smaller, less exciting, and actually true.

LTV:CAC ratio

Ratio = LTV ÷ CAC

A rough reading, assuming margin-adjusted LTV and fully-loaded CAC:

Below 1:1 losing money per customer   1–3:1 thin; fine while learning, not while scaling   3:1+ healthy   Above 5:1 often means you're under-investing in growth

CAC payback period

The number that decides whether you run out of cash

Payback (months) = CAC ÷ (Monthly revenue per customer × Gross margin %)

LTV:CAC tells you whether a customer is profitable eventually. Payback tells you how long your money is tied up getting there. A 5:1 ratio with a 24-month payback will starve a company that a 3:1 ratio with a 4-month payback would have funded comfortably.

Cohort retention

Not one number — a table. Group customers by the month they joined, then track what share remain in each following month.

What you're looking for: does the curve flatten? A curve that flattens at 30% means you have a real, durable customer base. A curve that keeps sliding toward zero means you have a leaky bucket, and no amount of acquisition spend will fill it.

Quick ratio

Quick ratio = (New + Expansion revenue) ÷ (Churned + Contraction revenue)

Growth efficiency in one number. Above 4 is strong for an early SaaS business; near 1 means you're running to stand still.

Email metrics

The core rates

Delivery rate = (Sent − Bounces) ÷ Sent × 100
Open rate = Unique opens ÷ Delivered × 100
CTR = Unique clicks ÷ Delivered × 100
CTOR = Unique clicks ÷ Unique opens × 100

CTOR is the copy-and-offer metric — of the people who opened, how many were persuaded. Subject line problems show in open rate; body and offer problems show in CTOR. Keeping them separate tells you what to rewrite.

List health

Unsubscribe rate = Unsubscribes ÷ Delivered × 100
Spam complaint rate = Complaints ÷ Delivered × 100
List growth rate = (New subs − Unsubs − Bounces) ÷ Total list × 100

Hard limit: major inbox providers expect bulk senders to keep spam complaints below roughly 0.3%, and treat 0.1% as the level to stay under. Cross it and your delivery collapses for every campaign, including the ones people wanted.

Revenue per recipient

RPR = Revenue from campaign ÷ Emails delivered

The metric that stops "send more" from looking like a strategy. Sending twice as often can raise total revenue while RPR falls and unsubscribes climb — you're harvesting the list faster, not growing it. RPR catches that; total revenue doesn't.

Open rate has been unreliable since 2021

Apple's Mail Privacy Protection pre-loads tracking pixels, so a large share of recorded opens never involved a human. Treat open rate as a relative signal between similar sends only, and never compare across the 2021 boundary. Optimise clicks and revenue instead.

SEO & content metrics

Non-brand organic traffic

Total organic traffic is a vanity number — it rises whenever your brand gets popular for reasons unrelated to SEO. Split branded from non-branded queries in Search Console. Non-brand organic is the only figure that measures the SEO work itself.

Organic CTR by position

Organic CTR = Clicks ÷ Impressions × 100 (per query or page)

Click-through falls steeply with rank — position one earns a large multiple of position five, and results below the fold earn very little. Ranking 3rd with an unappealing title can lose to ranking 5th with a great one, so compare your CTR against the average for your position before assuming you need to rank higher.

Share of search

Share of search = Your brand's search volume ÷ Total category brand search volume × 100

One of the few brand measures you can track weekly and for free. It tends to move ahead of market share, which makes it a genuinely useful early indicator of whether brand investment is landing.

Referring domains

Count unique linking domains, not total backlinks — a thousand links from one site is one relationship. Relevance beats raw authority scores, and those scores are third-party inventions, not signals any search engine publishes.

Content decay

Traffic to an individual page usually peaks and then declines as competitors update theirs. Audit quarterly: find pages down 20%+ year-on-year and refresh them. Updating an existing ranking page almost always returns more than writing a new one, and almost nobody budgets time for it.

Brand & satisfaction

Awareness

Unaided
"Name any brands in this category." Hard to earn, strongly linked to market share.
Aided
"Which of these have you heard of?" Easier, inflates quickly, less predictive.
Top of mind
The first brand named unprompted. The most valuable position in the category.

Share of voice

SOV = Your brand's presence ÷ Total category presence × 100

The related finding from advertising research: when share of voice exceeds share of market, brands tend to grow over time, and the reverse tends to shrink them. The gap between the two is called excess share of voice.

NPS — net promoter score

NPS = % Promoters (9–10) − % Detractors (0–6)

Watch out: NPS throws away the 7s and 8s, is heavily skewed by who you ask and when, and varies enormously by culture and category. It's fine as an internal trend line on a fixed methodology; it's nearly meaningless as a cross-company comparison — and it becomes actively harmful the moment someone's bonus depends on it.

CSAT & CES

CSAT = Satisfied responses ÷ Total responses × 100

CSAT measures a specific interaction. CES (customer effort score) asks how easy something was — and effort predicts churn better than satisfaction does, because people leave friction more reliably than they leave mild dissatisfaction.

Measurement hygiene

Most bad marketing decisions come from clean-looking numbers that were never comparable in the first place.

Six rules

  1. Write the definition down. "Active user" means nothing until someone specifies the window and the action.
  2. Never change a definition mid-comparison without restating history. Silent redefinitions destroy trust in the whole dashboard.
  3. Segment before you conclude. A flat average almost always hides one segment improving and another collapsing.
  4. Check the denominator. Most "conversion rate dropped" panics are traffic-mix changes.
  5. Beware small numbers. Going from 2 to 3 sales is "+50% growth" and also nothing.
  6. Pair every efficiency metric with a volume metric. CPA improves beautifully if you just stop spending.

Simpson's paradox, in one example

Campaign A converts better than B on mobile. A also converts better than B on desktop. Yet B has the higher overall conversion rate.

This is not an error — it happens whenever the two campaigns have different device mixes. It's the single most common way an aggregate number points in the opposite direction to the truth, and the reason "always segment" is the most valuable habit on this page.

Goodhart's law applies to every metric here

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Tie a bonus to MQLs and you will get MQLs — of steadily declining quality. The defence is to pair every target with a counter-metric that gets worse if someone games it: MQLs with lead-to-customer rate, CPA with volume, open rate with revenue per recipient.
